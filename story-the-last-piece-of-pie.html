The Last Piece of Pie

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 2 min read

🎧 Listen to this story

Every Sunday for thirty-one years, my mother cut the apple pie into five pieces. There were only four of us at the table.

The fifth piece went on the small blue plate. The one with the chip on the rim. She set it at the head of the table, in front of the empty chair, and nobody — not my sister, not my brother, not me — ever asked her to stop.

Dad had loved her pie. Not liked. Loved. He used to say a man could survive anything if Sunday ended with her pie and her laugh in the same room. When the heart attack took him in the spring of '94, we all assumed the fifth piece would stop by summer.

It never did.

For a long time, I thought it was grief holding a place at the table. I ate my piece quietly and tried not to look at his. When dinner ended, Mom would wrap that untouched slice in foil and put it in the fridge, and on Monday it would be gone. I never asked what she did with it. Some questions a son learns not to ask his mother.

Last spring, I came home early and found out.

It was raining. My truck broke down two streets over, so I walked in through the back door without calling first. Mom was at the table, alone, the fifth piece in front of her — and across from her, in Dad's chair, sat old Mr. Alvarez from down the road, eating slowly, with tears running down into his smile.

His wife had passed that winter. Every Sunday since, my mother had been carrying that piece of pie across the street after we all left, setting it in front of a man whose own table had gone quiet.

"He sits in your father's chair," she told me later, not embarrassed at all. "Because that's where the lonely people sit in this house. Right where they can be seen."

This Sunday, I did something I should have done thirty years ago.

I pulled out Dad's chair and sat down in it myself. Mom froze with the knife in her hand. Then she did the math of a mother's heart — and cut the pie into six pieces.

One for my sister. One for my brother. One for me, in Dad's chair. One for Mr. Alvarez, who arrived at five like always. One for her.

And one — still — on the small blue plate, at the corner of the table, for whoever might need it next.

"Your father used to say love leaves an empty chair," she said, setting it down gently. "He was wrong. Love doesn't leave. It just makes room."

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
