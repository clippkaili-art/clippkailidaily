The Wig

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 2 min read

The night my sister's hair started coming out, she called me from her bathroom floor, holding a handful of it like an injured bird, and said, "Well. That's that, then."

Chemo is a thief with a schedule. Week three, the oncologist had said. Week three, almost to the hour, Dana sat on the cold tile and made the call she'd been dreading, and I drove over with my husband's clippers in my purse and a plan I'd been rehearsing for days.

"Buzz it," she said, trying to sound like a general. "All of it. Tonight. On my terms."

So we did it the way we'd done everything since childhood — turned it into an occasion. We put on music. We opened the good ice cream. Our mother sat in the doorway because she couldn't watch and couldn't leave, which is the exact address of a mother's heart during something like this. And when the clippers had done their work and my beautiful sister sat there bald and brave and crying and laughing at her own reflection, I picked the clippers back up.

"What are you doing," Dana said.

"What does it look like?"

"Renée." She grabbed my wrist. "No. Absolutely not. It's just hair. You love your hair."

"I love my hair on your head," I said. "On mine, it's just hair."

I won't romanticize it: I cried at the first pass, and so did she, and then Mom cried, and then the dog hid. But by the second pass, Dana was laughing — really laughing, for the first time in the six weeks since the diagnosis — directing me like a film director: "Take it closer on the sides. If we're doing this, we're doing it fashionably."

Here's what nobody tells you about solidarity: it's not a gesture. A gesture lasts a photograph. Solidarity is everything that comes after the photograph — the months of itchy regrowth, the winter wind on a bare scalp, the strangers' stares you now share and split between you like a bill. Dana didn't go through one single stage of that year alone. Not the bald stage, not the fuzz stage, not the stage where it stuck straight up like a baby chick and made us both look deranged in every family photo.

Her last treatment was in March. Mine was never needed — I only ever needed the clippers, and I would pick them up again tomorrow, and she knows it, and that knowing is the whole point.

This spring, we got our hair cut together. A real salon. Real chairs. Her hair is a bob now, thick and defiant. Mine is past my shoulders again. We sat side by side in the mirrors like two girls in a bus seat sharing one song, and Dana reached over, mid-haircut, and took a strand of my hair between her fingers and said:

"We grew it back, Ren."

Inch by inch, is how you survive anything. Inch by inch, and never alone.
