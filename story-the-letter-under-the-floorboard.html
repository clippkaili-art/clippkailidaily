The Letter Under the Floorboard

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 3 min read

My sister and I didn't speak for six years, and my mother died believing it was her fault.

It wasn't. It was money, and pride, and one terrible Thanksgiving where things got said that can't be unsaid. She tried to fix it the way mothers do — phone calls, casseroles, "your sister asked about you" — but we were both too stubborn, and then she was gone, and the silence she'd tried to break outlived her.

We only ended up in the same room again because of the house. It had to be sold. Neither of us wanted a stranger's crew ripping through our childhood, so we did the repairs ourselves — two weekends of gritted teeth and separate ladders.

Anna found the letter on the second Sunday, under a loose floorboard in Mom's bedroom.

It was in an envelope with no stamp and no address. Just two words on the front, in Mom's handwriting:

"My babies."

We sat down on that bare floor, in the dust and the smell of old wood, and we read it together.

"My babies," it began. "If you're reading this, it means I never got brave enough to say it out loud, and I'm sorry for that too. A mother should say things with her mouth, not her floorboards.

"I need you to know something, because I watch you both carry it and it is breaking my heart from wherever I am. You have spent your whole lives trying to figure out which one of you I loved more. Anna thinks it's you, Michael, because I let you get away with everything. Michael thinks it's you, Anna, because I told you all my secrets.

"Here is the truth, and I want you to read it twice.

"I lied to both of you. I told each of you that you were the one I worried about most. Because each of you, in your hardest year, needed to believe you were first in somebody's heart. And you were. You were both first. A mother's heart is not a pie, my loves. It does not get smaller when you cut it. It is the only thing I ever owned that grew every time I gave it away.

"The house is just walls. The money is just paper. You two are the whole estate. Don't you dare divide yourselves."

At the bottom, she'd added a line in different ink, shakier, like it was written much later:

"P.S. Fight about anything else. Never about my love. There was always more than enough. There still is."

Anna read it three times. Then she looked at me with eyes I'd known since I was four years old and said the first true thing we'd said to each other in six years:

"She hid it under the floor, Mikey. She literally built this house on top of it."

We finished the repairs together. We did not sell the house. Anna's daughter lives there now, rent-free while she saves for her own, and every Christmas the whole family crowds into that little kitchen, and somebody always ends up telling the story of the letter.

Some letters are never sent because the words are too small.

My mother's were never sent because they were too big for an envelope. She had to hide them in the house itself — in the one place she knew her children would have to come back to, together, to find them.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
