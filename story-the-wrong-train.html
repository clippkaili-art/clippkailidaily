The Wrong Train

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 3 min read

My grandparents met because my grandmother got on the wrong train, and they have been grateful to that mistake every year for fifty-six years.

It was 1968. Grandma Rose, nineteen years old, was traveling to visit her sister and boarded the 4:10 to Millbrook instead of the 4:40 to Harrow Falls. She realized it three stops out, with her suitcase, her sandwich money already spent, and — in her words — "a full head of steam about it." She marched down the aisle looking for the conductor and found, instead, a young soldier asleep in the wrong seat, with his duffel bag taking up two more.

"You're in my seat," she announced, to the wrong man, on the wrong train, in the wrong town by then entirely.

Grandpa opened one eye. "Then I guess we're both lost," he said, and moved his bag, and gave her half his apple, and talked to her for two hours while the train took them both exactly where neither of them was going.

He walked her to the pay phone at the little station. He waited while she called her sister. Then he said the bravest sentence of his life, according to him: "Same wrong train, next Sunday. Just to make sure you get lost properly."

She came. Of course she came.

They married fourteen months later. And on their first anniversary, Grandpa surprised her: two tickets, the 4:10 to Millbrook. They rode it one stop, holding hands like teenagers, got off, crossed the platform, and rode straight back. "Our whole life started by going the wrong way together," he said. "Once a year, we honor it."

They have never missed a year.

Not the year the twins were newborns — they took the babies, and Grandma nursed one under a blanket while the conductor pretended to study the ceiling. Not the year of the blizzard, when the train ran four hours late and they ate vending-machine crackers on the platform and called it the finest anniversary dinner of their lives. Not the year Grandpa had his hip done — he rode with a walker, and the whole car applauded when he stood up at their stop "like a man half his age, which is to say, only forty years too old for that."

Last year, Grandma's memory started slipping. Small things first. Names. Keys. Why she'd walked into a room. In March, she looked at the calendar on the fridge, at the red circle around June 9th, and asked my grandfather what it was for.

He told her. Gently, the whole story — the wrong train, the apple, the pay phone.

She listened like it was a story about two other people. Then she smiled, sudden and bright as the girl on that train, and said, "Well. He sounds like a keeper. Did the girl keep him?"

"Fifty-six years," Grandpa said. "She's still deciding."

This June, they rode the 4:10 again. One stop out. One stop back. She held his arm and watched the fields go by, and somewhere past the river, she turned to him and said, clear as church bells: "This is our train, Henry."

It was. It still is.

Some love stories begin with fate. Theirs began with a mistake, a shared apple, and a nineteen-year-old girl in the wrong seat — and they have spent fifty-six years proving that the wrong train, taken together, was the right one all along.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
