The Treehouse 🌳

A Clipp Kaili original story

The treehouse had four names carved into the wood. There are only three of us.

I'm the middle child. Mark, forty-seven. My sister Lisa is fifty, my brother Tommy is forty-four. We grew up in that house in Ohio, and the oak tree in the backyard was our whole world — the treehouse Dad built in the summer of 1989, crooked and glorious, with a rope ladder and a trapdoor and a sign that said "NO PARENTS" in my wobbly nine-year-old letters. Dad pretended to be offended. He visited every single day.

Last month we finally sold the house. It took us two years to admit it was time. Mom's in assisted living now — her memory comes and goes like weather, sunny one minute and gone the next. Dad's been gone ten years. The house was too big, too empty, too full of echoes.

The day before closing, Tommy and I went back to say goodbye. We walked through every room like it was a funeral. My bedroom, where the glow stars were still on the ceiling. The kitchen, where Mom burned the Thanksgiving rolls in 1994 and we ate them anyway. The living room, where Dad fell asleep during every single movie.

We ended up in the backyard, staring up at the treehouse. It was gray and weathered and leaning a little to the left, and it was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen.

"I dare you," Tommy said.

"You're forty-four."

"And you're scared."

So we climbed up. The ladder groaned. The floor held. Inside, it smelled like old wood and rain. Our initials were still there, carved into the beams. The trapdoor still worked.

And there, carved into the far wall in Dad's careful block letters — the same letters from all his workshop labels — were four names:

MARK. LISA. TOMMY.

And underneath, smaller, in different handwriting. A child's handwriting, wobbly and earnest:

ELLIE.

Tommy went white. "Who the hell is Ellie?"

"I don't know," I said. But my hands were shaking, because I had a feeling — the kind of feeling you get when a memory is trying to surface and can't quite make it.

We searched the treehouse like it was a crime scene. Behind a loose board near the trapdoor, Tommy's fingers found a tin box — an old cookie tin, rusted at the edges. Inside: crayon drawings. A little girl's drawings. Houses with smoking chimneys. Suns with smiling faces. Stick figures holding hands — a tall one, a medium one, and a small one.

Every single one signed "Ellie" in wobbly letters. Dated 1984.

Five years before the treehouse was built. Two years before I was born.

"Maybe a neighbor kid," Tommy said, but he didn't sound sure, and he kept turning the drawings over like there'd be an answer on the back.

There wasn't. Just more crayon. More suns. More stick figures.

That night I went to see Mom. She was having a clear day — those are the ones that hurt the most, because for an hour you get her back, and you remember exactly what you're losing.

We talked about the house. About the closing. About the oak tree. And then I said, as casually as I could:

"Mom... who's Ellie?"

She looked at me for a long moment. The room went very quiet. Then her eyes filled up, and she said, very clearly, like she'd been waiting forty years to say it:

"We never told you about your sister."

I couldn't breathe. "My... sister?"

Mom took my hand. Her fingers were thin and cool, and her voice was steady — the steadiest I'd heard it in years, like she'd been holding these words so long they'd worn a groove.

"Ellie was our first," she said. "She was four. Bright as a button, your father used to say. She loved to draw — that's why the tin box. She drew all day long. Then one winter she got tired. Just... tired. We thought it was a bug. It was leukemia."

She paused. A nurse walked past the door. Mom waited until the hallway was quiet.

"Six months," she said. "From diagnosis to the end. Six months, and then our girl was gone, and your father and I didn't know how to be people anymore."

"Why didn't you tell us?" My voice cracked. "All these years — why?"

"Oh, honey." She cupped my face the way she did when I was small. "We tried. A hundred times. But then you'd come home from school and run straight to that treehouse, and you'd laugh up there with Lisa and Tommy, and it was just... yours. Your happy place. And we were afraid that if we told you, every happy memory would turn into a sad one. Every time you climbed that ladder, you'd think of her instead of just being a kid. So we let her be our secret. Our quiet one. We visited her every year on her birthday — just us two, at the cemetery. You probably thought we were running errands."

I thought about all those birthdays. All those "errands."

Tommy and I went back the next morning. We brought Lisa. The three of us stood in that crooked little treehouse — fifty, forty-seven, forty-four years old, squeezed into a space built for children — and we cried for a sister we never knew. And we cried for our parents, who carried it alone for forty years so we wouldn't have to.

Then Lisa did something I'll never forget. She took out her car keys, knelt by the wall, and scratched three words under Ellie's name in her own wobbly adult handwriting:

"FOREVER OUR SISTER."

We called the buyers that afternoon. "We have a strange request," I said. "Can we keep the oak tree? Just the tree — we'll fence off the corner of the yard, maintain it ourselves, never bother you."

There was a pause. Then the woman said, "We have kids. We understand. The tree stays."

Now there's a little brass plaque at the base of the trunk. It says: "Ellie's Treehouse. 1984. Forever our sister."

And every year on her birthday, there are five of us at the cemetery now. Not two.
