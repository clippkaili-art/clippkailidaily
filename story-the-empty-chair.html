The Empty Chair

A Clipp Kaili original story

At my graduation, there were three hundred folding chairs in the field house, and only one of them mattered.

It sat in the front row, on the aisle, with my mother's shawl folded over the back of it.

Mom made that shawl the winter I turned nine. She wasn't sick yet — or if she was, she hadn't told us. She just spent that whole winter by the window with her yarn, working on a shawl in my school colors, blue and gold, while I did homework at the kitchen table and complained about long division.

"This is for your big day," she said.

"Which big day?" I asked.

"Whichever one I need to be at," she said, "in case I'm running late."

I was nine. I thought she meant traffic.

She died when I was fourteen. The shawl went into a cedar chest with her wedding dress and a lock of my baby hair, and it stayed there for four years, because some things are too heavy to touch.

My father took it out the morning of my graduation. He didn't ask me. He ironed it — my big, quiet father, who burns toast, standing in the kitchen carefully ironing a woman's shawl like it was a flag — and he carried it into that field house and laid it over the front-row chair before anyone else arrived.

"I couldn't save her a seat with a ticket," he said. "So I saved her one the honest way."

I don't remember much of the ceremony. I remember the speeches blurring. I remember my name being called and the walk across the stage and the diploma in my hands. But before I went back to my seat — before the photos, before my friends, before any of it — I walked down to the front row.

I stood in front of that chair, in my cap and gown, holding my diploma with both hands, and I said, out loud, in front of three hundred people who all pretended not to hear:

"I did it, Mom."

Then I unfolded the corner of the shawl. And that's when I saw it — something my father hadn't known was there, something that had waited four years in the dark of a cedar chest.

Stitched into the hem, in blue thread, were six small words:

"Front row. Always. Love, Mom."

She had planned for a day she knew she might not see. Not with sadness. With thread. The way mothers love their children through every possible future, including the ones they won't be in.

My father keeps the shawl in his living room now, over the arm of his chair. People sometimes ask why there's a ball of yarn's worth of memory in a house with no knitter left in it.

The chair at my graduation was empty.

The love in it wasn't. It never has been.
