The Shared Headphones

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 2 min read

For eleven summers, my sister and I rode the same bus to our grandmother's house, and we owned exactly one pair of headphones between us.

One pair. Two kids. Four hours each way. You do the math on the arguments.

We solved it the way sisters solve everything — with a treaty. I got the left earbud, she got the right. The cord ran between us like a tightrope, and if one of us turned her head too fast, the other one got her ear yanked and the whole bus heard about it. We learned to move like we were one animal. Two heads, one song.

Maren chose the music. She was older, and she had a sixth sense for the exact song a moment needed. Rain on the bus windows? She had a song. The flat green fields going gold in late light? She had a song. The summer Dad was in the hospital and neither of us said a word for the first hour? She put on the old hymn Grandma used to hum, and we sat there holding the cord between us like a lifeline, crying quietly into our shared soundtrack.

"Why don't we just buy a second pair?" people asked, for years.

Because that was never the point, and we knew it even then. Two pairs of headphones is two people traveling. One pair is a sisterhood.

Then we grew up the way people do — not all at once, but in moves and marriages and time zones. Maren took a job in Oregon. I stayed in Ohio. We became the kind of sisters who text on birthdays and mean it when we say "we should call more," and the bus rides became a story we told other people, in the past tense.

Last spring, my phone buzzed at 6 a.m. A message from Maren: Made us a playlist. It's called Left Earbud. Press play at the same time as me. No skipping.

She had set a time. 7 p.m. her time, 10 p.m. mine. I made tea. I sat on my porch in the dark. And at exactly ten o'clock, three time zones away, my sister and I pressed play on the same song at the same second — the same song, I swear to you, that used to play when the bus crossed the river and the whole world went silver.

My phone rang before the chorus. "Are you at the good part?" she said.

"I just got there," I said.

"Don't turn your head," she said, "you'll yank my ear."

We listened to the whole playlist that way, talking between songs, quiet during the ones that mattered. It wasn't the bus. There was no cord. But distance, it turns out, is just another kind of bus ride — long, and dark, and better with your sister beside you.

We press play together every Sunday now.

Two states. One playlist.

Same song.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
