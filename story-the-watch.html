The Watch ⌚

A Clipp Kaili original story

My grandfather's watch stopped the day he died. It started ticking at my wedding.

His name was Walter. He raised me from the time I was eight, after the divorce — after my parents decided I was a custody arrangement instead of a kid, a weekends-and-holidays problem to be negotiated by lawyers.

Grandpa Walt didn't negotiate. He just showed up. Every Friday after school, his old truck would be in the driveway, and he'd honk twice, and I'd run out with my backpack. His house smelled like coffee and sawdust. He had a workshop, a garden, and absolutely no idea how to cook — we ate a lot of scrambled eggs and toast, and I loved every bite.

He taught me to fish. To change a tire. To shake a hand and look people in the eye. He came to every game, every concert, every parent-teacher conference. When I got cut from the basketball team in tenth grade, he took me fishing and didn't say a word for two hours, and somehow that fixed it. He was my dad in every way that counted. My real parents sent birthday cards. Walt sent himself.

The watch was his father's — a 1940s timepiece, gold, scratched, beautiful, with a leather strap worn soft as cloth. Walt wore it every day of his life. He'd wind it every morning while the coffee brewed — a little ritual, twist, twist, twist. "A man should know what time it is," he'd say, "even if he doesn't care."

When he died, two years ago — peacefully, in his sleep, at eighty-nine — it was on his wrist. And it had stopped.

At exactly 3:47. The time the nurse said he passed.

I took it to three watchmakers. The first two just shook their heads. The third — the best one in the city, an old German man with magnifying glasses perched on his nose — opened it up, studied it for ten minutes, and said, "Son, the movement is done. Finished. It's not worth fixing. Keep it for sentimental value."

So I did. I kept it in its little box on my dresser. Stopped at 3:47. Forever.

Last Saturday, I got married. To Anna — the love of my life, the woman Grandpa Walt never got to meet, though I told him all about her in his last months. I'd sit by his bed and show him pictures, and he'd squint and grin. "She's too good for you, Bobby," he'd say. "Don't mess it up."

"He'd have loved you," I told Anna once, early on.

"I know," she said softly. "I can tell. You're his work."

That morning, getting ready in the hotel room, my best man tying his tie wrong three times, I opened the little box. I don't know why. I just needed him close. I needed Walt there.

I put the watch on. Dead weight on my wrist. The hands frozen at 3:47, like always.

The ceremony was perfect. The little chapel was full. Anna walked down the aisle on her father's arm, and she was so beautiful I forgot how to breathe. We said our vows — mine were shaky, hers were steady — and right in the middle, right as the officiant said "I now pronounce you," right as I said "I do" —

Tick.

I froze. Anna saw my face change. "What?" she mouthed.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The watch was ticking. On my wrist. I could hear it — actually hear it, over the music, over the guests, over my own heartbeat.

I stared at it. The second hand was moving. Sweeping. Alive.

3:47. It still said 3:47. But it was TICKING.

I got through the rest of the ceremony in a daze — the kiss, the applause, the walk back down the aisle, all of it underwater. During our first dance, I held Anna close and whispered into her hair, "The watch is ticking."

She pulled back and looked at my wrist. Her eyes went wide. She heard it too.

After the reception, in the quiet of the hotel room, shoes off, bow tie undone, I took the watch off and held it up to the lamp.

It had stopped again.

At exactly 3:47.

I took it to the German watchmaker on Monday. I set the box on his counter and said, "It ticked. For hours. At my wedding."

He opened it up, examined it under his light for a long time, turning it this way and that. Then he closed the case, pushed it back across the counter, and frowned.

"Still dead," he said. "Movement's shot. Same as before. It cannot tick."

"But it did," I said. "My wife heard it too."

He looked at me for a long moment over those magnifying glasses. Then his face softened, and he said something I'll never forget:

"Son... some things don't run on gears."

I keep the watch in the box again. On my dresser. Stopped at 3:47.

But I know what I heard. Anna heard it too — she tells everyone, unprompted, at every dinner party. "During our vows," she says, "during our first dance — his grandfather's watch was ticking. Dead for two years, and it ticked."

Walt promised me something, once. I was maybe twelve. We were fishing at the lake, the sun going down, the water like glass. Out of nowhere I asked him — kids ask the big ones when you're not ready — "Will you be at my wedding someday?"

He was quiet for a while, reeling in slow. Then he said, "Bobby, I plan to be at everything. Every game, every graduation, your wedding, all of it." Another pause. "And if I can't be there in person — I'll find a way. That's a promise. Walt promises don't break."

He found a way.

Now Anna's pregnant. It's a boy — we just found out. We're naming him Walter.

And on the day he's born, I'm going to put that watch on. Wind it, even though winding does nothing. Just in case.

Because Walt promises don't break.
