The Note in the Book

A Clipp Kaili original story

The library book was due back in 1994. Somebody returned it thirty years later — with a note inside that saved my senior year.

I found it because our small-town library was clearing its oldest shelves, and volunteers could take one discard book home. I chose the worn copy of a novel I'd never heard of because the cover was coming off and nobody else wanted it. That felt familiar. I was seventeen, my parents were splitting up in the loud, slow way, and I was failing at acting like it wasn't happening.

The note fell out on page 112.

It was written on a piece of notebook paper, folded small, in purple ink that had faded to the color of old violets. No name at the end. Just a date: March 1994. And this:

"If you're reading this at the part where she loses everything — keep going. I almost stopped here. My mom was sick and I was so angry at this book for pretending people survive things. They do. I'm writing this from the other side of the worst year of my life. It didn't fix. It changed. There's a difference, and the difference is enough. Whoever you are: finish the book. Then finish your year. — R."

I read that note nine times at my kitchen table.

Then I did something I'd never done. I started writing back — not to send, just on notebook paper of my own, one page for every bad day. R., my dad moved out today. R., I got a C and cried in the car. R., you were right about chapter fourteen.

The book became the one place I told the truth that year. Every other room in my life required a performance: fine at school, fine at Dad's apartment, fine at Mom's table with the empty chair. But R. had told the truth first, thirty years before I needed it, and left it in a book like a rope thrown forward in time.

I graduated. Not with honors. With honesty, which was harder.

This spring, our library held a reunion for its volunteers, and I brought the book — I'd never been able to give it back. I told the room about the note. An older woman in the second row went very still. Afterward she came up to me holding a coffee with both hands, like it was keeping her upright.

"Purple ink?" she asked.

My heart stopped.

"My name is Ruth," she said. "I worked the desk here all through high school. My mother died the winter after I wrote that. I put notes in eleven books that year. It was the only way I knew to talk to anybody. You're the first person who ever—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

We stood in that library and cried between the history stacks, a woman of forty-eight and a girl of eighteen, holding the same battered book — two worst years, thirty years apart, holding each other up.

I keep a fresh note in the book now. It's in blue ink, on the last page, unsigned.

It says: She was right. It changes. Finish your year.

The book is back on the shelf, where it belongs. Some books you don't own. You just carry them to the next reader.
