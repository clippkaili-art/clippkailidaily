The Suitcase Notes

A Clipp Kaili original story

The night before I left for college, my mother zipped my suitcase shut herself and said, "Don't open the pockets until you need them."

I laughed. "Mom, I need everything in there tomorrow."

"No," she said. "You'll know when you need them."

She was standing in my bedroom doorway with her arms crossed, trying very hard to look casual, the way she had been trying all week — helping me fold sweaters, buying me extra socks, making jokes about finally getting her quiet house back. We both knew neither of us believed the jokes.

The first pocket I opened was the front one, the night I arrived, because my charger was missing and I was sure she had packed it. She had. Under it was an envelope in her handwriting: "For your first night, when the room feels too quiet." Inside was a card with three lines: "You are not far. You are just started. Call me after you eat something real. Love, Mom."

I sat on that narrow dorm bed and read it four times.

Over the next four years, I found them all — because she had hidden one in every single pocket, every zippered corner, every fold of that suitcase. One in the shoe pocket: "For the first bad grade. One grade is a day, not a life. Come home Friday, I'll make soup." One in the side pouch: "For when money runs low and you're too proud to ask. There's twenty dollars taped inside this card. Asking is not failing." One tucked behind the lining that I did not find until junior year: "For when a boy makes you feel small. You were never small. Leave."

Each note arrived exactly when I needed it, which is a trick only mothers can do — knowing in August all the weathers their child will walk through by spring.

But there was one pocket I never opened. The small one inside the lid, the one she pointed to that last night in my doorway. "That one," she said, "is for the homesick day. Not a sad day. The real one. You'll know."

The real one came in October of my senior year. Nothing terrible had happened. That was the worst part. I was just tired, and grown, and standing in a grocery store an hour from home crying because the apples smelled like our kitchen in fall. I drove back to my apartment, pulled the suitcase down from the closet shelf where it lived, and opened that pocket with shaking hands.

Inside was not a card. It was a key. Our house key, the spare from under the mat, taped to a note that said:

"Homesick means you had a home worth missing. This door does not lock against you. Not at nineteen, not at ninety. Come home whenever. I'll put the kettle on. — Mom"

I drove home that night. She opened the door in her robe, no surprise on her face at all, and put the kettle on before I had even taken off my coat.

My daughter leaves for college next August. Her suitcase is already in my closet.

I have started writing the notes.
