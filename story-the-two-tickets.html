The Two Tickets 🎫

A Clipp Kaili original story

Every Friday at 6:14 PM, my seventy-eight-year-old father stands on Platform 4 holding two train tickets.

And no one ever comes to meet him.

I first heard about it three months ago, when Mrs. Delgado from next door mentioned it over the fence in that voice people use for bad news.

"Your father," she said quietly. "Every Friday. Same platform. Two tickets. He just... stands there."

I laughed it off. Dad was fine. Dad was sharp as ever.

But the following Friday, I drove past the station at 6:20.

There he was.

Gray coat. Two tickets in his left hand. Eyes fixed on the empty track.

My stomach dropped straight through the floor.

Was he losing his mind? Was this how it started — the forgetting, the confusion, the slow goodbye?

I didn't confront him. I did something worse.

I followed him.

The next Friday, I parked across the street and watched. At 6:05, his old Buick pulled into the station lot. At 6:10, he bought two tickets from the machine — I watched him press the button twice, deliberately, like it mattered.

At 6:14, he took his place on Platform 4.

The 6:14 to Millbrook rolled in. The doors sighed open. People stepped off.

Nobody came to meet him.

He didn't look disappointed. He just nodded at the young conductor — who clearly knew him by name — and the conductor punched both tickets without a single question.

Both of them.

The conductor — his name tag read "Marcus" — caught my eye through the windshield and smiled like this was the most normal thing in the world. Later I would learn he had been punching my father’s second ticket for two years. "He always says the same thing," Marcus told me. "She’s just running a little late."

Then Dad sat on the bench, placed the second ticket carefully on the empty seat beside him, and stared down the track until the train pulled away.

I sat in my car and cried like a child.

The next morning, I called my sister. "Have you noticed anything... strange about Dad lately?"

A long silence. "You mean the station."

"You knew?"

"For months," she said softly. "I thought if I ignored it, it would stop."

It didn't stop. Every Friday. Steady as a heartbeat.

So I did what any worried daughter would do.

I went through his desk.

I told myself it was concern, not snooping. I told myself a lot of things that morning.

In the bottom drawer, under a stack of old tax returns, I found a shoebox.

Inside: dozens of used train tickets. Every single one stamped FRIDAY. Every single one punched for two passengers.

Some were so old the ink had faded to ghosts. I counted back through the years — through my college graduation, through my wedding, through the birth of my own daughter. Every Friday. He had never missed one. Not one.

And at the very bottom, a photograph — the edges soft with sixty years of handling.

My parents. Young and laughing. Standing on Platform 4, holding up two train tickets like they had just won something precious.

On the back, in my mother's handwriting: "Our first Friday. 1968."

My mother died three years ago.

Three years of Fridays. One hundred and fifty-six pairs of tickets. While the rest of us were busy with our lives, my father had been keeping the most faithful appointment in the world.

I sat on the floor of my father's study and held that photograph until my hands stopped shaking.

That evening, I drove to the station. Not to follow him this time.

To wait for him.

He came out at 7:30, surprised to see my car at the curb.

"Honey? What are you doing here?"

"Dad," I said, my voice barely holding together. "Tell me about the tickets."

His face did something I had never seen in seventy-eight years of knowing him.

It crumpled — just for a second — before he carefully put it back together.

"Your mother hates being kept waiting," he said.

And then he told me about 1968.

In 1968, my father was a nervous twenty-two-year-old who had finally worked up the courage to ask a girl named Ruth on a Friday date.

"She said yes before I even finished the sentence," Dad told me, smiling at the memory. "We took the 6:14 to Millbrook. There was a little diner by the station. We split one milkshake with two straws."

They never stopped going. Every Friday. For fifty-five years.

Through the wedding. Through two kids. Through the lean years and the good ones.

"The train was our place," he said. "No phones. No bills. Just us and the world going by the window."

When Mom got sick, they still went — he pushed her wheelchair right onto Platform 4, and the conductors always saved them the bench.

The last Friday, three years ago, she was too weak to leave the house.

"She held my hand," Dad said quietly, "and whispered, 'Same time next week, Ruthie.'"

She died on a Tuesday.

"That Friday, I went anyway," he said. His voice didn't waver once. "Bought two tickets. Sat on our bench. And I swear to you, honey — I could feel her there. Laughing at me for being sentimental."

He looked at me then, and his eyes were clear as morning water.

"I'm not confused. I'm not forgetting anything. I know she's gone."

He held up the two tickets between his fingers.

"But love doesn't check the schedule, kiddo. She'll be along. She's just running a little late."

I didn't argue. What was there to argue with?

The next Friday, I went with him.

We stood on Platform 4 at 6:14. He bought three tickets this time — pressed the button three times, slow and deliberate.

The young conductor punched all three and winked at me.

Dad placed Mom's ticket on the bench between us, exactly where it had always gone.

And as the 6:14 pulled away into the purple evening, my father put his arm around my shoulders and said the words I will carry for the rest of my life:

"She'd be so glad you're here. She always said Fridays were for family."

Some people wait on platforms for trains.

My father waits for love.

And now, so do I.
