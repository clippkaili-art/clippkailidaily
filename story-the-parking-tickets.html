The Parking Tickets

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 3 min read

My mother found the shoebox the spring after her treatment ended, on the top shelf of the hall closet, in my father's handwriting: WORTH IT.

Inside were parking tickets. Dozens and dozens of them, from St. Mary's Hospital — a full year of them, dated from the January of her diagnosis to the December morning the doctor used the word "remission." Every appointment. Every scan. Every long day of treatment that started in the dark and ended in the dark. My father had kept every single one.

"Mom," I said, holding the box like it might spill. "Did you know about this?"

She sat down at the kitchen table and went through them one by one, the way other women go through love letters. Because that is what they turned out to be.

On the back of each ticket, in pencil, in Dad's small square writing, was the day.

"Jan 9 — First treatment. She was scared. So was I. She held my coat sleeve the whole drive home."

"March 3 — Bad blood work. We got pie anyway."

"May 22 — She laughed today. The nurse said that's medicine too."

"Aug 30 — Eight hours. She slept. I watched her breathe and thanked God for every single one."

My father is not a talking man. In fifty years of marriage, my mother has been the voice of their house — the stories, the phone calls, the prayers said out loud. We used to tease that Dad's love language was showing up: oil changed, porch fixed, driveway shoveled before she woke. He loved her in verbs, never in sentences.

Except on the backs of parking tickets.

The one on the very bottom of the box was from the first day, the diagnosis day, the worst day. He must have written it in the car while she was still inside getting dressed, because the pencil is shaky.

"Dec 11 — They said the word. She cried in the parking lot so nobody inside would see. Whatever this costs, it costs. She's worth it."

That was the label on the box. Not a summary. A verdict he'd reached on day one and never once revisited through a year of meters and machines and four-dollar hours that added up, we calculated later, to more money than their first car.

Mom held that first ticket a long time. Then she got up, found a pencil, and turned over the last one — the December remission day, the only one with a blank back.

She wrote, in her own hand: "Dec 18 — He never missed a day. Not one. I got well in a hospital, but I got healed in a passenger seat. Still worth it. So is he."

The box is back on the shelf now. Both handwritings are in it.

My parents never wrote each other love letters.

They didn't have to. A hospital parking lot kept their correspondence — proof, stamped and dated, that love is mostly just paying to stay close to someone, hour after hour, until the hard year ends.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
