The Long Drive Home

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 2 min read

My brother and I hadn't spoken in four years when our father died and left us a truck neither of us wanted and both of us refused to let the other one sell.

The pickup was a 1998 Ford, rust at the wheel wells, a bench seat cracked down the middle, and 212,000 miles of our father's life in it. The plan — if two men who communicate through a probate lawyer can be said to have a plan — was simple: drive it from Dad's place in Missouri to a buyer in Colorado, split the money, and go back to our regularly scheduled silence.

Jake drove first. I rode shotgun with my arms crossed, watching three states' worth of nothing roll by. We made it ninety miles on radio and resentment.

Then the truck started teaching us its curriculum.

At a gas station outside Salina, Jake reached into the glove box for the registration and came out with a cassette tape labeled, in Dad's handwriting: BOYS — LONG TRIPS. "He made us a mixtape from beyond the grave," Jake said, and something in his face cracked. We played it. Dad's voice, recorded who knows when, doing terrible impressions of the radio announcers from our childhood road trips. Jake laughed so hard he had to pull over. I hadn't heard my brother laugh in four years. I'd forgotten it started with a snort he was embarrassed about. I wasn't embarrassed. I wrote it down in my heart like evidence.

In the bed of the truck, under a tarp, we found his fishing poles. Two of them. His — and the spare. "He always brought the spare," Jake said quietly. "In case somebody changed their mind about coming."

Somebody. Me. I was the somebody who stopped coming.

We talked that night at a motel with a buzzing sign, the way men in our family talk — sitting side by side, facing a parking lot, never looking at each other, which is how the truth gets out. The will. The farm. The things said at Mom's funeral that had calcified into four years. It all came out somewhere between the second cup of gas-station coffee and midnight, and none of it, said out loud, was as big as it had been in the silence.

We reached Colorado on the third day. The buyer walked around the truck, kicked a tire, and offered us less than it was worth.

Jake looked at me. I looked at Jake.

"Not for sale," we said, at the same time, and then stood there grinning like idiots in a stranger's driveway.

We drove it home. Both our names are on the title now. It lives at Jake's place, and every few weeks my phone buzzes: Truck's running. River's up. Brought the spare pole.

Some inheritances you split.

Ours, we had to share — one mile at a time, until we remembered how.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
