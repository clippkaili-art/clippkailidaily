The Last Summer ☀️

A Clipp Kaili original story

We sold the lake cabin. Then the new owners called about the walls.

The cabin was in our family for forty years. My parents bought it in 1986 — a little two-bedroom on the water, with a dock that leaned and a fireplace that smoked. Every summer, all of us: me, my three siblings, Mom, Dad, and eventually a small army of grandkids. Swimming off the dock. Bonfires with s'mores. Dad's terrible fish stories — the one about the twelve-pound bass got bigger every single year until the fish was practically a whale.

Dad died last spring. Seventy-eight, heart failure, peaceful. At the funeral, Mom held my hand and said, "I can't keep the cabin without him, David. Every room is him." So we sold it. It hurt like a tooth being pulled, but it was time. We all said so.

Last month — our final summer there — we'd done it anyway. One last July, all of us, even though Dad was gone. We swam. We built the bonfire. My brother Mike told the twelve-pound bass story, and we all corrected him the way Dad used to: "It was EIGHT pounds, Mike!" We cried a lot. We laughed a lot. On the last day, we walked through every room and said goodbye out loud, like idiots, like it could hear us.

Or so we thought.

Two weeks after closing, my phone rang. Unknown number, local area code.

"Hi, is this the Miller family? This is Greg — we bought your cabin." He sounded nervous. "Listen, we're doing some renovations — opening up the living room wall — and we found something. You should probably come see this."

My heart stopped. "What did you find?"

"Envelopes. Four of them. Old. With your names on them. In your dad's handwriting, I think."

I called my siblings. Carol cried on the phone. Mike went silent — which for Mike means a lot. Steve just said, "I'll drive." We went up that weekend — all four of us, something we hadn't done in years.

Greg met us at the door, looking sheepish and kind. He led us to the living room, where a section of wall was open, studs exposed, insulation pulled back. And there, tucked between the studs in a neat row, were four yellowed envelopes, held together with a rubber band that crumbled when Greg touched it.

Mine said "DAVID." My sister's said "CAROL." My brothers': "MIKE" and "STEVE." Dad's handwriting — unmistakable, that slanted all-caps.

"We didn't open them," Greg said quickly. "Didn't feel right. They've been in there a while, though. The contractor says that wall hasn't been touched since the nineties."

We sat on the floor of our old living room — someone else's living room now, smelling like fresh paint — and opened them together. Four adults, sitting cross-legged like kids.

Each envelope had a letter. Dad's handwriting. Dated ten years ago — 2016, the summer Mom turned seventy.

Mine began: "David, if you're reading this, the cabin is sold, and I'm gone, and you're probably sitting on the floor..."

We laughed through tears. Classic Dad — he knew us. He knew exactly how this would go.

Each letter was personal. Mine was about the year I almost quit college and he talked me off the ledge over pancakes at a diner. Carol's was about her divorce, and the things he'd never said to her face because "fathers are cowards about the big feelings, honey." Carol sobbed. Mike's was about the bass story — the real one, the eight-pound truth — and Mike had to leave the room.

But Steve — my youngest brother — his envelope was different.

No letter. Just a brass key. And a slip of paper with an address, in Dad's hand.

None of us recognized the address. Dad had never mentioned it. Not once. Not in forty years.

"What is this place?" Carol whispered, wiping her eyes.

I typed it into my phone. Forty minutes away. I zoomed in on the map.

According to the satellite view, it was...

...a storage facility. Forty minutes from the cabin, out by the highway. Unit 114.

We drove there in silence — four cars, one after another, like a funeral procession. Steve's hands were shaking as he fit the brass key into the padlock. It turned. The door rolled up with a rattle.

Inside: a wooden canoe. Half-restored. Dad's tools laid out beside it on a workbench, exactly where he'd left them — sandpaper, varnish, clamps, a child's life jacket hanging on a nail.

And taped to the bow, a note in Dad's slanted all-caps:

"Kids — I was fixing her up for the grandkids. Ran out of summers. You know what to do. Finish her together. That's an order. — Dad. P.S. Steve: you're in charge. Try not to sink her."

Steve laughed until he cried — that awful, wonderful sound.

So that's what we did. All summer, every weekend, the four of us — plus nine grandkids, ages four to sixteen — drove to that storage unit and worked on Dad's canoe. The kids sanded (badly). We varnished (better). Carol painted the name on the bow in her best hand-lettering: "ONE MORE SUMMER." Mike's teenage son wired a little waterproof speaker into the stern, "for Grandpa's oldies," he said, very seriously.

In August, she was done — gleaming, beautiful, smelling like varnish and pine. We called Greg. "Can we launch her? One time? On the lake?"

He didn't just say yes. He brought his whole family down to the dock to watch, with lemonade. "This lake's about families," he said. "That's why we bought here."

Nine grandkids piled into that canoe, wearing life jackets, screaming with laughter. Mike's son played the oldies. And as "ONE MORE SUMMER" floated out onto the water — forty years of summers in one little wooden boat — I swear I could hear Dad's voice, clear as the lake air:

"See? Told you I'd get you back on that lake."

We sold the cabin. The walls are someone else's now.

But Dad made sure we'd always have one more summer.
