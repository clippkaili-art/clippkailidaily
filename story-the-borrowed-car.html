The Borrowed Car

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 3 min read

My mother didn't sell me her car. She lent it to me in 2016, and we both pretended for nine years that I was going to give it back.

It was a silver sedan with a dent in the rear door where she had met a mailbox in 2009 and a radio that only held one station clearly — the oldies station, which she considered a feature. She got a newer car when she retired, and she handed me her keys and said, "Just until you're on your feet, baby." I was twenty-six, working two jobs, and on my feet was a place that took a while to reach.

Every Mother's Day I offered to start paying her for it. Every Mother's Day she waved me off. "She's paid for," she'd say. "And she likes you better. Cars know."

She named the car, of course. Doris.

Doris carried me to a better job, then to a wedding, then home from the hospital with a baby seat in the back that my mother installed herself, twice, because "the man at the store did it fast, and fast is not the same as right." Doris's odometer rolled past two hundred thousand miles with my whole adult life happening inside her.

This spring, Doris finally gave out — gently, in my driveway, with a sigh and a puddle, the way very old and well-loved things do. The mechanic said the words "not worth fixing," and I nodded and cried in his waiting room like we were at a funeral, which, in a way, we were.

I cleaned her out myself before the tow. The trunk. The back seat. The glove box.

The glove box was full of receipts. Every oil change, every tire rotation, every inspection for nine years — services I had paid for myself, I thought, at the garage near my apartment. But these receipts were from her garage, across town. The dates were the same as mine. And I understood, sitting in that passenger seat with my hands full of paper, that every time I had taken Doris in and been told "no charge today, ma'am, it's covered," my mother had called ahead and covered it. Nine years. Without ever once mentioning it.

On every receipt, in the margin, in pencil, in her handwriting, she had written the same five words for the mechanic to keep in the folder:

"She'll take care of you."

I thought she meant the car. The mechanic, who came out and found me crying and understood immediately, said, "No, ma'am. She told us that was for you. Every time she called, she said the same thing — 'Whatever it costs. She'll take care of you means I already did.'"

My mother, when confronted, was completely unrepentant. "A mother is a pit crew, baby," she said. "You don't announce a pit crew. You just make sure the car keeps running."

I bought a new car last month. Nothing fancy. But before I drove it off the lot, I took one of those receipts out of my wallet, where I keep it now, and I taped it inside the new glove box.

"She'll take care of you."

My daughter turns sixteen in six years. I am already saving. And I already know — I will lend her this car, and we will both pretend, and the glove box will be full before she ever thinks to look.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
