The Banner

A Clipp Kaili original story

By Clipp Kaili Daily · October 29, 2026 · 3 min read

The banner said WELCOME HOME in blue paint that has faded for thirty years — and last month, we hung it for the person who painted it.

My grandmother made it in the summer of 1994, on a white bedsheet, with fabric paint and total seriousness. My cousin Danny was coming home from his first deployment, and Grandma declared that a soldier should see his welcome from the end of the driveway, not hear about it later. She painted each letter big enough to read from the road. DANNY asked if all this fuss was necessary. Grandma said, "A homecoming you have to ask about isn't one."

The banner went up across the front porch, tied to the railings with twine. Danny stood in the driveway in his uniform with his duffel at his feet and read it three times before he came up the steps. Then it was folded — Grandma insisted on folding it herself, a particular way, paint inward — and put in a cedar chest in the hall.

It came out for my sister's first baby, for my brother's graduation at dawn, for Uncle Ray after his heart surgery, and for me, once, after the worst year of my life, when I came home with my belongings in my car and no plan at all. The banner was already up. Grandma was already on the porch.

You are expected. You are celebrated. The door was counting the days.

Thirty years. Fourteen homecomings, by Grandma's own count, kept in pencil on the back of the sheet near the hem, each one dated in her small, careful hand. Soldiers, babies, graduates, the healed, the retired, the lost-then-found. The paint cracked. The twine got replaced. The ritual never did.

This spring, Grandma fell and broke her hip, and for eleven days the house was quiet in a way that scared all of us. Ninety-one years old, and the woman who had welcomed everybody home spent slow weeks learning to walk her own hallway again. It was Danny — the first name on the back of that sheet — who finally typed what we were all thinking.

"Get the banner."

We hung it the morning she came home. It took four of us and two ladders. The paint is paler now, but the letters are still big enough to read from the end of the driveway.

She saw it from the corner.

The car stopped. We watched her read it, the way Danny had, three times. Then her hand came up to the window, flat against the glass, and she laughed — that surprised, delighted sound none of us had heard since before the fall — and she cried and scolded us in the same breath for making a fuss.

"You made the fuss first, Grandma," Danny said. "Fourteen times. We counted."

She looked up at the banner over her own porch, at the whole noisy family underneath it.

"Well," she said. "It's about time it was my turn to be expected."

Some families hang a flag to say who they are.

We hang a bedsheet — because in this family, coming home is the whole religion, and everybody, eventually, gets welcomed like it.

This story is a work of fiction, written to touch hearts and bring families closer.
