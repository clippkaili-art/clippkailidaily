The 5 A.M. Call

A Clipp Kaili original story

For one full year, my grandmother called me at five o'clock every single morning — and I mean every morning, including the one after her own hospital stay.

I was eighteen, three states away, in my first year of college, and I had a problem no alarm clock had ever solved: I slept like the dead. Two alarms. A roommate shaking me. Nothing worked for an eight a.m. class that my entire degree depended on. I mentioned it to Grandma Rosa on a Sunday call, laughing, the way you mention a flaw you think is permanent.

Monday, 5:00 a.m., my phone rang.

"Up," said my seventy-nine-year-old grandmother, wide awake as a farmer. "Feet on the floor. Tell me when they're on the floor."

"Grandma. It's five."

"Your class is eight there, it's eight here, and your brain needs three hours to become a person. I know you, Anthony. Up."

She called Tuesday. She called Wednesday, through a thunderstorm that knocked her power out — she called from her neighbor's porch. She called on Thanksgiving, on Christmas morning, on the day of my grandfather's birthday, which I know cost her something. She called the morning after she spent a night in the hospital with chest pains she had told none of us about until later. The nurse said she'd asked for her phone at 4:45 a.m. like it was medication.

We never talked long. Sometimes it was two minutes. "Feet on the floor. Eat something with protein. Your mother loves you. Be good." Sometimes she stayed on while I made coffee, telling me the news of her street — who was feuding, whose tomatoes had come in, what the preacher said that she agreed with and the longer list of what she didn't. Those mornings, I was not a lonely freshman in a concrete dorm three states from home.

I was just a boy in his grandmother's kitchen, at a distance.

My grades came up. My mornings found a rhythm. By spring I was waking at 4:58, two minutes before the call, watching the phone, smiling in the dark like an idiot. She knew. "Listen to that," she said. "The boy's cured. My work here is almost done." She called anyway, every day, until the last morning of finals.

"You'll call Sundays now," she said. "Like a normal person. And Anthony — don't go telling people your grandmother woke you up for college. Tell them you showed up. That's the part that was you."

I graduated last May. Grandma Rosa is eighty-three now, and her mornings are slower, and these days I'm the one who sets an alarm.

So on graduation morning, I called her at 5:00 a.m. sharp.

She answered on the second ring — awake, of course, she'd been awake since four, too excited to sleep. And I said the words in her own order, the liturgy of my entire first year away from home:

"Feet on the floor, Grandma. Eat something with protein. Your grandson loves you. Be good."

She laughed, and then she cried, and then she said, "Well. Somebody raised you right."

Love doesn't always travel to you.

Sometimes it just calls at five in the morning, every morning, until you learn how to rise on your own.
